Exploring Genres Through Albums: Eight Starting Points

Published November 2, 2026 by Shaaroo Games

Most people's music taste is narrower than they think, and it's rarely a matter of preference. It's a matter of entry points. If you've never listened to reggaeton, K-pop or 1960s rock, it's hard to know where to begin, and a "best of" playlist or a genre's biggest hit doesn't always help.

Albums are a better doorway. A single album gives you a complete, curated picture of an artist's sound, and a good one is built so that you hear the genre's strengths in a sensible order. Here are eight albums, from the catalog in Order the Album, that make good starting points for different genres, along with what to listen for.

How to explore an unfamiliar genre

Before the list, a short method that works for almost any genre:

  1. Play the album once in full and in order. Don't skip the songs that don't grab you. You are trying to learn the genre's sound, not curate a playlist.
  2. Notice what repeats. Genres are defined by repeated choices: a particular rhythm, a recurring instrument, a typical song structure. Try to name two or three.
  3. Pick one track you liked and find out why. Was it the beat, the voice, the lyrics, the production?
  4. Follow the thread. Look up who produced or featured on the tracks you liked, and move outward from there.
  5. Try the album in the game. Rebuilding the tracklist forces you to listen closely in a way passive listening doesn't.

1. Pop: Future Nostalgia by Dua Lipa (2020)

With 11 tracks, Future Nostalgia is short and focused, and it's a very clean example of modern pop built from disco and 1980s dance influences. Notice how nearly every track has a strong rhythmic identity and a hook that arrives early. The album opens with its title track and delivers its big singles ("Don't Start Now," "Levitating") in the first half, which is a classic pop strategy.

2. Alternative R&B: Blonde by Frank Ocean (2016)

Blonde has 17 tracks and is a good example of how far R&B has moved from the verse-chorus template. Listen for the spaces: guitars rather than drum machines, vocals that are pitch-shifted, spoken-word interludes, and songs that end sooner than you expect. It rewards slow, headphone listening. If you like it, Channel Orange (2012) is a slightly more traditional, soul-influenced companion.

3. Hip-hop: 2014 Forest Hills Drive by J. Cole (2014)

This album has 13 tracks and tells a loose story about growing up, coming of age and returning home, and the title refers to the address of Cole's childhood house. It's a strong example of narrative rap: listen to how the order builds from reflection toward resolution, and how the songs between the singles do much of the storytelling. It's also an album people often mention because it did well with no guest appearances.

4. Latin pop and reggaeton: Un Verano Sin Ti by Bad Bunny (2022)

With 23 tracks, this is a big album, and a good one to see how wide "reggaeton" actually is. It's anchored in Caribbean rhythms but travels through a range of moods, from beach-party songs to slower, more reflective ones. The tracklist is organized around a feeling of summer, from "Moscow Mule" at the start to "Callaita" at the end. If you'd like something denser and more varied, YHLQMDLG (2020) has 20 tracks and includes "Safaera," a track famous for shifting through several distinct sections.

5. K-pop: Love Yourself: Tear by BTS (2018)

K-pop albums are often built around concepts: a theme, a visual identity and a storyline that spans several releases. Love Yourself: Tear has 11 tracks and opens with an intro and closes with an outro, a structure BTS uses often. Listen for the way the group moves between rapping and singing, and how the production switches between dance-pop and moodier, more experimental tracks. For a shorter, more compact introduction to the genre's pop side, try BLACKPINK's Born Pink (2022), which has 8 tracks.

6. Classic rock and pop: Rubber Soul by The Beatles (1965)

Rubber Soul has 14 tracks and is often described as the point where the band began treating the album, not the single, as the main unit. It's worth hearing as a snapshot of how pop songwriting expanded in the mid-1960s, with folk influences and more personal lyrics. If you like it, the next step is Revolver (1966), which pushes further into studio experimentation.

7. Country-influenced pop: Cowboy Carter by Beyoncé (2024)

This 27-track album is a big, deliberate genre crossover: country, but also folk, rock, opera and more. It's a useful case study in how a genre's boundaries get argued about, and it's framed with interludes that present the album as a kind of radio show. Notice how it reworks existing songs, including a cover of Dolly Parton's "Jolene" and a version of The Beatles' "Blackbird" (listed as "Blackbiird" in the tracklist).

8. Dance and house: Renaissance by Beyoncé (2022)

Renaissance has 16 tracks and draws on house, disco and club music. It's a good one for understanding dance music's structure, because it plays like a DJ set: tracks flow into each other and the energy rises and falls over the whole record. If you've only heard dance music as isolated singles, hearing it as a continuous piece is a very different experience.

What to do next

Pick the one that feels the most unfamiliar to you, not the one you're sure you'll like. Play it start to finish. Then try rebuilding the tracklist in the game. If you can't remember the order, that's useful information: it means the album did something you weren't expecting.
